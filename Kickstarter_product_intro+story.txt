LIVESAT — THE SATELLITE WEATHER WATCH
Real-time weather from space. On your wrist. No phone required.


THE SHORT VERSION

LiveSat is a standalone wearable that downloads live satellite imagery
directly from NASA, plays 24 hours of weather as an animation on a
2-inch AMOLED screen, tracks hurricanes, displays moon phases, and
works anywhere in the world — all without a phone, a subscription, or
a cell connection. Just WiFi and your wrist.

No product like this exists. We built one.


WHY THIS EXISTS

I wanted to see the weather from space. Not a forecast — the actual
satellite view. NASA operates three geostationary satellites 22,000
miles above Earth that photograph the entire hemisphere every ten
minutes. That imagery is public. It's free. And nobody had put it on
a wearable.

I started with a website — a 3D globe showing real-time satellite
weather from all three NASA satellites, blended seamlessly where their
coverage overlaps. 15,000 lines of code. 41 test suites. It worked.

Then I thought: what if you didn't need a computer?

I moved it onto a microcontroller the size of a postage stamp with a
tiny screen. It connected to WiFi, downloaded satellite frames, and
played them back as a looping animation. Bare circuit board, wires
everywhere — but it proved the concept could run on real hardware.

Then I built the real thing.


WHAT LIVESAT DOES

The device plays a continuous display loop:

  - 24-HOUR SATELLITE ANIMATION — Watch a full day of weather unfold
    across the hemisphere. See storm systems develop, fronts move,
    hurricanes form. This isn't a static snapshot — it's the actual
    motion of the atmosphere over the last 24 hours.

  - CLOCK FACE — Time and date, because it's still a watch.

  - ZOOM SEQUENCE — The view dives from the full hemisphere down to
    your local area, showing terrain, radar overlay, and moon phase
    along the way.

  - GLOBAL COVERAGE — The device knows where it is and automatically
    switches between NASA's three satellites: GOES-East and GOES-West
    for the Americas, Himawari for Asia and the Pacific. Take it
    anywhere in the world and it adapts.

  - HURRICANE TRACKING — During storm season, LiveSat monitors active
    tropical systems and alerts you automatically. No checking an app.
    No refreshing a page. It just tells you.

  - MOON PHASE — Calculated and displayed on every cycle. For anyone
    on the water, this matters — tides, visibility, and navigation all
    depend on the lunar cycle.

  - SHAKE TO WAKE — Flip your wrist like checking a regular watch and
    it wakes up. Set it down and it sleeps to conserve battery. The
    motion sensor knows the difference between a deliberate wrist-check
    and ordinary arm movement.

  - BUILT-IN WEB PORTAL — Pull up a page on your phone's browser to
    check the device's status, battery level, and stored imagery.

All of this runs on a chip you could cover with a quarter.


WHY NOT AN APP?

A reasonable question. Two reasons:

First, Apple's App Store terms restrict apps from pulling data directly
from government servers like NASA's GIBS imagery service. Android has
similar limitations. A dedicated device sidesteps platform gatekeepers
entirely — it connects to the satellites on its own terms.

Second, and more importantly: independence. An Apple Watch weather app
requires an iPhone, an Apple ID, a cellular plan if you want standalone
operation, software updates, app management, and a proprietary charger.
LiveSat has one button and a touchscreen. Charge it, connect it to WiFi
once, and it handles everything from there. No accounts, no
subscriptions, no phone in your pocket.

For someone on a boat, on an oil rig, on a remote island — that
simplicity isn't a feature. It's the point.


HOW IT COMPARES

The closest existing product is RainViewer, an Apple Watch app that
shows weather radar and satellite imagery. It's built by a company of
11-50 employees, founded in 2017, with nine years of development behind
it. Here's how LiveSat differs:

  LIVESAT                              RAINVIEWER
  ──────────────────────────────────   ──────────────────────────────────
  Full-hemisphere satellite animation  Local radar/satellite tile
  24-hour weather playback             ~2-hour radar history
  No phone required                    Requires Apple Watch ($250+)
  No cell connection needed            Needs WiFi or cellular on watch
  Automatic hurricane tracking         Severe weather alerts (via Apple)
  Moon phase display                   No moon phase
  Global auto-switching (3 sats)       Regional coverage
  One-time purchase, no subscription   Free app, but $250+ hardware
  Works at sea with just WiFi          Needs cell/WiFi infrastructure


THE DEVICE

  Display:      2.06" AMOLED, 410x502 pixels
  Processor:    ESP32-S3 dual-core, 8MB PSRAM
  Storage:      MicroSD (satellite frames cached locally)
  Sensors:      6-axis IMU (wake-on-motion), real-time clock
  Power:        LiPo battery with AXP2101 PMIC, USB-C charging
  Connectivity: WiFi 802.11 b/g/n
  Software:     10,500 lines of custom C++ firmware
  Data source:  NASA GIBS (GOES-East, GOES-West, Himawari)

THE STORY

I'm Travers. I'm a software engineer who, until January 2026, had almost
no experience with hardware programming. I knew Python, JavaScript, web
development — but not C++, not microcontrollers, not how to talk to
sensors and manage batteries on a chip.

I built LiveSat anyway. Two months, 10,500 lines of C++, and a lot of
late nights later, it works. Along the way I solved problems that would
challenge experienced embedded engineers — a shared pin that controls
both display power and the motion sensor, an SD card library that
silently corrupts data if you don't handle flush timing exactly right,
a motion sensor whose interrupt behavior contradicts its own
documentation.

I built this with the help of AI tools — Claude and OpenAI's Codex —
across 63 separate development sessions totaling over 3 gigabytes of
conversation. But AI doesn't design products or make engineering
decisions. It answers questions. The architecture, the problem-solving,
and the product vision are mine.

By March, an independent AI evaluation of my ten major programming
projects rated me 9/10 overall — "Staff-Level Engineer," comparable to
professionals with 8-12 years of experience. My C++ skills went from
6/10 to 8/10 in two months. I say this not to brag, but because you
deserve to know the engineering behind this device is serious.

I'm now moving to the British Virgin Islands to run fleet maintenance
for a charter company. I'll be living in the middle of LiveSat's target
market — on the docks, among the captains and crews and sailors who
need this most. This isn't a project built in a lab. It's built by
someone who's about to rely on it every day.

RISKS & CHALLENGES

Every hardware project has risks. Here's what could go wrong and how
we're handling it:

  MANUFACTURING DELAYS — Chinese manufacturers quote 8-16 weeks for
  custom enclosure tooling and production. We've built buffer into the
  timeline. The Explorer Edition ships immediately because it uses
  off-the-shelf components that are already in hand.

  WATERPROOFING — Sealing electronics is hard. We're working with
  established watch manufacturers in Shenzhen who do this at scale,
  not trying to reinvent it ourselves. Prototype testing before
  production commitment.

  FIRMWARE UPDATES — The software is mature (10,500 lines, months of
  daily use), but bugs happen. LiveSat's web portal supports over-the-
  air configuration, and the firmware can be updated via USB-C. Backers
  won't be stuck with launch-day software.

  SCOPE — We are not building a smartwatch. LiveSat does one thing
  exceptionally well: satellite weather on your wrist. We're not
  adding fitness tracking, notifications, music, or apps. The
  simplicity is the product.

FAQ

  Q: Does it need my phone?
  A: No. LiveSat is fully standalone. It needs WiFi to sync imagery
     (about once every 6 hours), but between syncs it runs entirely
     on its own.

  Q: How often does it update?
  A: Configurable. It can sync every 15 minutes, every hour, or on a
     custom schedule. Between syncs, it plays back the most recent
     24 hours of cached satellite frames.

  Q: What about battery life?
  A: With shake-to-wake enabled (screen off when not in use), the
     device runs for [TBD — depends on sync frequency and usage].
     It charges via USB-C.

  Q: Can I use it outside the Americas?
  A: Yes. LiveSat automatically switches between GOES-East, GOES-West,
     and Himawari based on your location. Take it anywhere.

  Q: Is the satellite data free?
  A: Yes. NASA's GIBS imagery service is public and free. There is no
     subscription, no data fee, no ongoing cost. You buy the device
     once and it works.

  Q: Is the firmware open source?
  A: The Explorer Edition ships on open hardware (Waveshare ESP32-S3
     dev board) and the firmware will be available to Explorer backers
     for modification. Pro firmware updates will be provided as
     compiled binaries.

  Q: Why not just use Windy / RainViewer / Weather Channel?
  A: Those apps show local radar tiles on a phone or smartwatch. They
     require a phone, a cell connection, and a platform. LiveSat shows
     full-hemisphere satellite animation on a standalone device with
     no dependencies. Different product, different use case.

  Q: What if the campaign doesn't fund?
  A: If we don't hit $25,000, no one is charged. The Explorer Edition
     will still be available for direct purchase outside Kickstarter,
     as it uses off-the-shelf components with no tooling required.
